*A quick note: this is an anecdotal guide based on club pilot experiences, and should not be used as a substitute for official sources.*

New York, New York. Is there anything more magical than seeing the world's most iconic city from the air? Seeing it from the air at sunset, of course! Or perhaps seeing it from the air at night.

Wondering how you can do this? It's easier than you might think. If you're a licensed pilot, student, aspiring to become one, or just generally interested in flying, read on.

## Two ways to fly the Hudson

- **Below the Class B, in the Hudson River Exclusion.** No clearance needed. You fly under your own navigation and self-announce on a common frequency.
- **Through the Class B, on the Skyline Route.** You get a clearance from ATC and fly above the Exclusion, at 1,300 ft MSL or higher, with ATC providing separation.

Which one to take? It's a hotly debated topic, and different pilots have different opinions. Some prefer the Exclusion because no clearance is required and the view feels a little closer. Other pilots prefer the extra set of eyes ATC provides and like being away from the traffic below.

There are of course a bunch of rules for flying the Bravo and the Exclusion, both written and unspoken, which we will cover here.

## Before you begin: brush up your radio skills

Regardless of whether you choose to self-announce on the CTAF or talk to ATC, all the frequencies in the area can get very busy. It's advisable to study the map and rehearse your calls in advance to get them as succinct as possible.

Most pilots and controllers operating this airspace do it every day, saying the same things over and over, and they've developed a little bit of shorthand.

Listening to the livestream on [LiveATC.net](https://www.liveatc.net) (stations: KEWR, KLGA, KJRB (CTAF)) or ATC.com (EWR / LGA Class B (Surface / VFR)) can also help you build a picture of what to expect.

Besides, what pilot doesn't love sounding cool, calm and collected on the radio?

## The Hudson River Exclusion

Ok so, what exactly is the Hudson River and East River Exclusion Special Flight Rules Area (SFRA), aka "the Exclusion"?

At a glance, the New York City chart looks like three giant blobs of Class Bravo airspace over Newark, LaGuardia and JFK. Look more closely, and you'll notice that over the water between the Verrazano-Narrows Bridge in the south and Alpine Tower in the north, there is a carve-out from the surface to 1,299 ft MSL. This is the Exclusion.

It's uncontrolled airspace, and pilots in the Hudson portion self-announce on CTAF 123.05. The East River section is generally only permitted for use by helicopters and floatplanes, with ATC approval.

### The Exclusion has two layers

- **Transient:** flying the full length from one end to the other, at 1,000 ft MSL up to the Class B floor. This is the layer for most fixed-wing pilots passing through. Don't climb, descend or make big heading changes.
- **Local:** anything else below 1,000 ft MSL, including sightseeing, circling and helicopter tours. Expect aircraft climbing, descending, turning and flying slowly.

### The rules (14 CFR 93.351 and 93.352)

- Keep your indicated airspeed at **140 knots or less**.
- Turn on **anti-collision and position lights**. Landing lights are recommended.
- **Keep right:** fly along the **west (New Jersey) shoreline southbound** and the **east (Manhattan) shoreline northbound**.
- Carry a **current New York TAC** (or the New York Helicopter Route Chart) and know what's on it.
- **Self-announce** at each charted mandatory reporting point, giving aircraft type, position, direction and altitude, on the frequency shown on the TAC.

### Reporting points and frequency

The CTAF at time of writing is **123.05**, and the reporting points, from south to north, are:

| Reporting point | Say |
|---|---|
| Verrazano-Narrows Bridge | "VZ" |
| Statue of Liberty | "Statue of Liberty," "Statue" or "the Lady" |
| The clock on the west bank (Colgate Clock, Jersey City) | "Clock" |
| Intrepid Sea, Air & Space Museum | "Intrepid" |
| George Washington Bridge | "GWB" |
| Alpine Tower | "Alpine Tower" |

An example call: *"Skyhawk, VZ, one thousand one hundred, northbound."*

### Circling the Statue of Liberty

- The FAA recommends doing it as a local operation. Fly the river as a transient (1,000 to 1,299 ft) past the Verrazano-Narrows Bridge, then come back in lower.
- Circle **counterclockwise**, at the highest practical altitude below 1,000 ft.
- This is the busiest spot in the corridor. It's where tour helicopters turn around, at about 500 ft, and where many climb into the Class B. Keep your eyes peeled, listen to the radio, and make regular announcements, e.g. *"Skyhawk, Statue, 900 ft, orbiting counterclockwise."*
- Newark's airspace begins above 500 ft MSL just west of the Statue, so don't drift west while circling.

### East River Exclusion: not for planes

- Airplanes may not fly the East River Exclusion, from Governors Island to Roosevelt Island, unless they're seaplanes landing or taking off, or LaGuardia Tower has authorized it (14 CFR 93.353).
- Authorization is rarely practical for a sightseeing flight. Turn back, continue up the Hudson, or obtain a clearance to fly up the East River in the Bravo instead.

## Flying in the Bravo (the Skyline Route)

- **What it is.** The FAA's name for flying the Hudson *in* the Class B, at 1,300 ft MSL or higher, with an ATC clearance. You're under ATC control and separated from other traffic, above the helicopters and the busy Exclusion.
- **Why fly it.** Less traffic to watch for, a higher and safer altitude over the city if the engine quits, and arguably a better view.
- **Which frequency to call.** Watch out! It's not the first Tower frequency published on the charts. Both Newark and LaGuardia have a separate Tower controller for the surrounding airspace in a ~6 mile radius from the airport, also known as the helicopter position. From the south, you'd typically contact Newark on **127.85**. Coming from the north, you'd typically talk to LaGuardia on **126.05**. During quiet periods, it may be combined with the primary Tower frequency.
- **The catch.** ATC grants it only when workload allows, so have a plan for flying the Exclusion instead. Expect to be given an altitude and route, and to fly exactly that.
- **How to ask for it.** Report your callsign and position, and request *"Hudson northbound"* (or southbound) or *"the Skyline."*
- **Remember "Class B clearance."** You aren't in until you hear *"cleared into the Bravo."* Until then, remain outside.

### Can I fly over Central Park?

Yes, if LaGuardia Tower can fit you in, as their Class B sits right over it. From the Hudson, ask for an eastbound Central Park transition to the East River. You can make the request while already in the Bravo or from the Exclusion (a useful second chance if you couldn't get a Bravo clearance initially). Wait for an explicit Class B clearance before climbing into the Bravo.

If you're coming from the south and want to go up the East River first, then cross west to the Hudson, you need to pass your whole request to LaGuardia Tower on 126.05 before reaching Governors Island.

## Before you go

- **TFRs.** Presidential and VIP TFRs over Manhattan are common, especially during the UN General Assembly in September. Check NOTAMs on the day.
- **Weather.** The Class B floor caps you at 1,299 ft, so you need a ceiling comfortably above that. Haze and wind over the river can make it harder than it looks.
- **Training.** Take the FAA's free online Hudson River SFRA course on [FAASafety.gov](https://www.faasafety.gov).
- **Passengers and photos.** Brief passengers to stay quiet near reporting points and to help spot traffic. Decide in advance who's taking pictures. It isn't the pilot.
- **The aircraft.** Both club aircraft have the Garmin GTN 650Xi, which helps with situational awareness in the corridor. {{< todo "Any club tips: autopilot use, which plane members prefer for photos" >}}

### Congested-area minimum safe altitudes (§ 91.119): a quick refresher

You might spend most of the flight over water, but what if ATC routes you over a congested area like Staten Island or Manhattan? Before accepting the route, check that the assigned altitude gives you at least 1,000 ft of clearance above the highest obstacle within 2,000 ft horizontally of your aircraft.

The pilot in command is responsible for meeting that minimum, not ATC. If you can't comply at the assigned altitude, tell ATC "unable" and request a higher altitude or a different route.

Section 91.119 allows you to fly below the minimum when necessary for takeoff or landing. Whether a lower altitude is necessary depends on the departure or approach you're flying and the performance of your airplane.

## Flying it from Linden

A typical round trip takes about 1 Hobbs hour. See what that costs in a club aircraft in [Flying club vs. renting near NYC](/guides/club-vs-renting-near-nyc/).

**Sunset and night flights** are the perfect time to take your friends, family and loved ones! Many of our members have flown the Hudson at each phase of the day, to compare and contrast the experience. Each has its own pros and cons.

- **Plan as usual.** Check NOTAMs and ATIS and conduct your normal flight planning. Occasionally, the relevant Hudson ATC positions are unstaffed, and this will be posted in a NOTAM or an ATIS message.
- **Beware the Bravo.** Linden sits just under the Newark Bravo, with an 800 ft lower limit. This means low patterns, and can also make Linden harder to spot from the air. There is not much margin for error.
- **Caution: choppers.** Linden is a base for many helicopters. Be mindful of their presence and flying characteristics.
- **Requesting the Bravo on departure.** A typical call: *"Newark Tower, Skyhawk 123 out of Linden, requesting the Skyline, we have [ATIS]."* A typical response: *"Skyhawk 123, Newark Tower, squawk 4321."* Once you've read back and entered the code, you'll likely hear something along the lines of: *"Skyhawk 123 identified, [position], cleared into the Class Bravo via the VZ, Hudson northbound, VFR 1,500."*
- **Head to the VZ.** If you're flying the Exclusion, this will be your entry point. In our members' experience, most Bravo clearances to and from Linden are routed via the VZ as well. Be sure to adjust your course to the south to avoid clipping the Bravo shelf if you don't have a clearance.
- **Shortcuts over Staten Island.** ATC may on occasion offer a shortcut to or from Linden over Staten Island, often using the Bayonne Bridge as a landmark. However, it is not uncommon to be instructed to descend to or maintain 800 ft several miles from Linden, to ensure separation from heavy traffic departing and arriving at Newark. If you are not careful, you may suddenly find yourself overflying a congested area at 800 ft MSL for much longer than necessary (now you see why we mentioned § 91.119 earlier!). If you're not comfortable with your routing, request higher, a reroute via the VZ, or stay a little further south, underneath the next Bravo ring, which will allow you to cross the island comfortably above 1,000 ft.
- **Flying north on the Hudson.** Continue to fly the Exclusion or follow ATC's instructions, and keep an eye out for traffic (put your passengers' eyes to use too). Bravo flyers: ATC will usually ask how far north you plan on going, so have your answer ready.
- **Two options to turn back:**
  1. A course reversal on the Hudson, most commonly performed near the George Washington Bridge (GWB).
  2. Across Central Park (requires a Bravo clearance), then head south along the East River.

{{< photos "george-washington-bridge.jpg" "yankee-stadium-aerial.jpg" >}}

- **Handoff to LaGuardia (Bravo flyers).** Just after you pass the southern tip of Manhattan, Newark will typically hand you off to LaGuardia. You can then let them know your callsign, altitude and intentions.
- **Southbound leg.** Whether you are returning south on the Hudson or the East River, you'll typically be handed back to Newark, workload permitting. Be prepared to descend into the Exclusion if they're unable to accommodate you, and switch to the CTAF, 123.05.
- **Orbiting the Statue in the Exclusion (optional).** This is perhaps the part where you need to be most alert, and where the FAA course materials are especially relevant. Lights and radio calls are important. Given the Statue is on the western side, if you plan to make an orbit, it makes more sense to do it on your southbound return leg.
- **Requesting a Statue orbit in the Bravo.** *"Tower, Skyhawk 123, requesting an orbit of the Statue, then back to Linden."* This is typically approved at 1,500 ft, followed by an instruction to head to the VZ. If you're flying a high-wing aircraft like the Cessna 172, a higher orbit can sometimes be better, as the wing is less likely to block the view of the Statue as you bank around it. Another small benefit of orbiting in the Bravo is that you can orbit clockwise, giving your passengers a clearer view.
- **To the VZ, then back to Linden.** Back at the VZ, you can start your turn back toward Linden once clear of the Class Bravo and the Exclusion. If you flew the Bravo, ATC will usually end radar service here. Occasionally, they'll keep you in the Bravo and assign a westerly heading across Staten Island (see the note on shortcuts above) toward Linden.
- **The smoke stack.** A useful visual reference on your return leg is the single 518 ft smoke stack of the Arthur Kill power station, on the western edge of Staten Island, across the river from Linden.
- **Spotting Linden.** Linden is small and easy to miss from the air, especially when you're flying low beneath the Bravo shelf. As you get to know the area, landmarks like the clusters of oil storage tanks, I‑95, the Rahway River and the solar farm can help you pick out the airport. Before flying in on your own, consider a familiarization flight with a pilot who knows the area, especially if you plan to arrive at night.
